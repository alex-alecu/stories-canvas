# Stories or videos for children under 5? The truth is more nuanced than "screens are bad"

For children under 5, the best question is not only "how many screen minutes?" It is also **what the child is doing during that time, with whom, and what the screen is replacing**.

A video can be educational. A digital story can be beautiful. But neither replaces the things a young child needs most: sleep, physical play, exploration, conversation, and connection with an adult.

The World Health Organization recommends no sedentary screen time for children under 2, and for children aged 2-4, no more than one hour per day of sedentary screen time, with less being better. In the same guidance, WHO encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver during quiet time. ([World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550536))

## Why stories have a real advantage

Stories, especially when read or listened to together with an adult, have a major advantage: they are slow. The child has time to ask, notice, repeat words, stop at a picture, and imagine what comes next.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends shared reading from birth, not only for language, but also for the warm relationship between child and adult during a critical period of brain development. ([AAP Publications](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/154/6/e2024069090/199467/Literacy-Promotion-An-Essential-Component-of))

This is the important difference: **a story is not just content; it is conversation**. When a parent asks, "What do you think the little bear will do now?" or "How does the girl in the story feel?", the child practices language, empathy, attention, and thinking.

Harvard Center on the Developing Child describes these exchanges as "serve and return": the child points, asks, reacts, and the adult responds. This back-and-forth supports language, social skills, and brain architecture. ([Harvard Center on the Developing Child](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/key-concept/serve-and-return/))

## Why videos can become a problem

Videos are not automatically bad. High-quality educational programs exist, and children aged 2-5 can learn from good content, especially when a parent watches with them and talks about what is happening. The AAP recommends limiting screen use for ages 2-5 to about one hour per day of high-quality content, with co-viewing. ([AAP Publications](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/138/5/e20162591/60503/Media-and-Young-Minds))

The problem is more common with fast, highly stimulating videos: autoplay, rapid cuts, constant rewards, and very little room for the child to pause or think. A study in Pediatrics found that just 9 minutes of a fast-paced cartoon temporarily affected executive function in 4-year-olds, including attention, self-control, and following simple rules. ([Pediatrics](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/128/4/644/30924/The-Immediate-Impact-of-Different-Types-of))

There is also the "video deficit": young children generally learn less well from video than from direct interaction with an adult. Reviews of children aged 0-6 show that learning from video is weaker than face-to-face learning, although the gap decreases with age and adult support. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33491209/))

In short: video can be useful, but young children need context, limits, and an adult nearby. Left alone with autoplay, it can easily become passive consumption.

## Digital stories are still screen time. How we use them matters

An AI-generated story with images and voice can be a useful tool. But it is still a digital experience. The healthy difference comes from how it is used.

A digital story is most useful when:

- the child reads or listens with an adult;
- the adult asks questions and connects the story to the child's life;
- the images are calm rather than overstimulating;
- there is no endless autoplay;
- the session is short;
- the story does not replace sleep, outdoor play, or traditional reading.

On Magic Stories, a healthy use might look like this: a parent creates a 5-10 minute story about a real situation, such as starting kindergarten, fear of the dark, or sharing toys, then reads it with the child. At the end, the phone or tablet is put away and the story continues in play: "Now let's be the dragon and the rabbit."

That turns AI from a digital babysitter into a starting point for connection.

## Sleep matters: less screen in the evening

For young children, screens before bed can affect sleep. Studies on children's screen use before sleep show associations with later bedtimes, shorter sleep duration, and more night waking. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11581737/))

That is why the better evening option is usually a story read by a parent, a printed book, or audio without the child staring at a screen. If you use a digital story, it is best to choose it earlier and read it calmly.

## A simple rule for parents

For children under 5, the format is not magic. A poor book does not become good just because it is printed. A good video does not become bad just because it is on a screen. But on average, stories have an advantage: they are easier to turn into conversation, imagination, and connection.

A practical rule:

**Videos for short, carefully chosen moments. Stories for connection, language, and imagination. The adult remains the main ingredient.**

Technology can help. AI can create personalized stories, images, and voices. But for a young child, the most important function is the same as it has been for thousands of years: an adult who stays close, listens, responds, and says, "Let's see what happens next."
